# Writing & exam guidelines

Condensed from Prof. Feest's guidance on seminar papers and exam
handouts/Thesenpapiere, used across several courses in the program (not a
verbatim reproduction — see your course's Stud.IP for the full documents).

## Seminar papers

- **Start from a clearly scoped question.** State the topic, question, and
  goal in the introduction; keep the goal realistic for a seminar paper
  (e.g. "analyze a specific problem in Kuhn's model," not "develop a theory
  of scientific development").
- **Have a thesis**, usually stated at the end of the introduction.
  Distinguish an *interpretation thesis* (your reading of what a text
  claims) from a *discussion thesis* (your evaluation of that claim, which
  can go beyond the text).
- **Substantiate both.** For an interpretation thesis, back it with textual
  evidence (quotes, with page numbers). For a discussion thesis, pinpoint
  precisely where the problem lies — inconsistency, ambiguous terms, flawed
  premises, an invalid inference — and explain why. Watch the scope of your
  own claims: disproving one argument for X doesn't show X is false.
- **Suggested structure:** introduction → textual analysis (reconstruct the
  author's thesis/argument, not a full summary) → discussion (your own
  thesis, situated against the text) → summary and outlook.
- **Sources:** ground your interpretation in primary and secondary
  literature (encyclopedia overviews like the *Stanford Encyclopedia of
  Philosophy* are a good starting point), but always cite — quoted text in
  quotation marks with a precise reference, paraphrases also referenced.
  Author–date citation (Chicago style) is the convention in philosophy of
  science.
- **Formal requirements:** cover page (name, matriculation number, degree
  program, title, professor, module, date), bibliography, plagiarism
  statement; Times New Roman or Calibri, 11–12pt, double-spaced; roughly
  12–15 pages.
- **Revise with distance.** Once you know what you actually want to say,
  go back and rewrite the introduction to match — and have someone else
  read it for clarity, philosopher or not.

## Handouts / Thesenpapiere (presentations & oral exams)

- **Purpose:** not a table of contents, not a full script — a compact set
  of claims you'll develop live.
- **Structure:** header (name, DOB, matriculation number, degree program,
  module/seminar and instructor, date) → title/topic → one prose paragraph
  introducing the topic → 4–6 numbered theses → 3–4 references (≥1
  primary, 1–2 secondary — only texts you actually used to prepare).
- **What counts as a thesis:** a non-obvious, defensible claim — not a bare
  fact or a section-heading phrase. "Douglas argues that inductive risk
  makes non-epistemic values an ineliminable part of the scientific
  process" is a thesis; "The Value-Free Ideal" is not.
- **Formal requirements:** 11–12pt, 1.5–2.0 spacing, max 2 pages, numbered
  theses, Chicago-style references. For an oral exam, send the handout at
  least 5 days ahead for review.
